Ten minutes apart

A polar orbiter gives you a place twice a day. A geostationary satellite gives you the same place every ten minutes — so the clouds in one frame are the clouds in the last frame, moved.

GOES-East and GOES-West hang over the equator and photograph the same disk continuously. That makes them the only free archive where a cloud field can be watched becoming another cloud field, rather than sampled as unrelated skies. Everything below was pulled from NASA GIBS at no cost.

The left panel goes dark and fills with city lights while the right panel carries on unchanged — same clouds, same minute, different physics. Infrared measures cloud-top temperature, so it cannot tell day from night. That is the argument for training a temporal model on infrared rather than on true colour.

A third rendering, Air Mass, was fetched and dropped: it carries a static no-data mask across the southern third of every scene here — measured at 53–78% of the southernmost tiles, while GeoColor over the same ground is clean. That is a property of the product, not of the fetch.

What a model can learn from this that it cannot learn from tiles

A direction in latent space

Train on pairs ten minutes apart and the difference between their latent codes is, on average, one direction: the way weather moves. Walking that direction generates the next sky rather than a sky.

Motion, not just texture

A still tile teaches what a cumulus field looks like. A sequence teaches that it advects downwind while it grows — the shape and its tendency are different facts, and only one of them is in a single frame.

The diurnal cycle

Andes and Gulf were chosen for it: cloudless morning, convection by afternoon, collapse at dusk, every day. That is the strongest periodic signal in the archive and the easiest to check a model against.

What it holds, and what it cost

The archive: €0

NASA GIBS serves these tiles free and unauthenticated. The whole pull ran on a laptop over an afternoon; the only cost is bandwidth. GIBS keeps roughly the last month at ten-minute cadence, so the window rolls — frames older than that are gone unless we hold them.

The temporal model: about €6

Fine-tuning the existing 256 px cloud network on frame pairs, then fitting one direction in W from the pairs, is a few hundred kimg on an L40S at €1.47/h. The expensive part of this project was never the data.
