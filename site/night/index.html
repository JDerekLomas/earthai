The real night

Fourteen nights of the Earth after dark as it actually was: moonlit cloud, city lights, fishing fleets, fires, and on the stormy nights the aurora.

A polar-orbiting satellite crosses every place on Earth once a night, at about half past one local time, and its Day/Night Band is sensitive enough to photograph the ground by moonlight. This page stitches one night's passes onto a globe, one still per night, and steps through the last two weeks. It is not a film: every strip of the picture is a different moment, and each place appears exactly once.

What you are looking at

The night side is a photograph. NOAA-20 flies over each place at about 01:30 local solar time and the Day/Night Band records whatever light there is: cities, gas flares, wildfires, fishing boats lit to draw squid, moonlight on cloud and snow, the faint glow of the upper air itself, and the aurora. It is one greyscale channel, shown here with a curve that lifts the moonlight without blowing out the city cores. The day side is drawn, not photographed: nothing on this satellite sees the ground in daylight in this band, so the lit crescent is NASA's Blue Marble under a sun we placed, there only so the globe reads as a globe. Switch it off and the whole sphere is the real night.

Because every place is seen once, the picture for a date is really a ribbon of moments. NASA composites the passes that fall in one UTC day, so the picture dated the 8th shows the small hours of the 8th over the Americas and the night that began on the evening of the 8th over Europe, Asia and Australia; the two ends of the day meet near 22°E, in a thin sliver that one satellite misses and the other fills. Where the night never gets dark enough, the Arctic in September, there is no picture at all, and the Not photographed button shows exactly where the drawing begins.

The nights, and how stormy the sky was

Kp is the planetary index of geomagnetic disturbance, measured every three hours from magnetometers around the world, 0 to 9. At Kp 5 there is a geomagnetic storm and the auroral oval, which is always there over the Arctic and Antarctic, brightens and pushes towards the equator; around Kp 7 it reaches the northern United States. The bars are the eight readings of each UTC day; a night is marked when any of them reached 5. Moonlight is the other thing that changes the picture from night to night: near full moon the clouds show like a black-and-white photograph, near new moon only the lights.

The aurora

What is honest and what is not

the night side

One sensor, one curve

Every night-side pixel is the Day/Night Band's own radiance, from the same NASA rendering, through the same fixed curve. Nothing is painted in: not the clouds, not the lights. The curve is a black point and a gamma chosen by eye so that a moonlit night and a moonless one both read; it is the same on every night, so a brighter night on this page was a brighter night.

the day side

Drawn, and declared

The lit crescent is a basemap lit by an invented sun. It carries no weather and belongs to no date. It is there because a globe with no day side does not read as a planet; the all night button removes it.

the swaths

A ribbon of moments

The satellite's swath is about 3,000 km wide and the strips lean with the orbit. Adjacent strips are a hundred minutes apart, so a front can jog at a strip edge, and the edges of a strip see the ground at a slant and look slightly brighter. Where NOAA-20 left a gap, the older Suomi NPP, on the same orbit a few minutes apart, fills it; the fill is shown under Not photographed.

the ends of the Earth

Twilight

Poleward of about 70° the September night never gets dark and the band has no picture; that cap is the basemap, dimmed. Around Antarctica the band does see, but in twilight, so the sea ice and cloud there are far brighter than anything else on the night side, and the southern auroral oval sits inside that glow.
