The whole Earth, every ten minutes

Three days of the real atmosphere, all of it at once, from the five satellites that hang over the equator.

Five geostationary satellites each photograph their own side of the planet every ten minutes, day and night. This page stitches those pictures onto one globe and plays them back at four hours a second. Drag to turn it, or just watch. Switch to sun-fixed and the daylight stands still while the Earth turns underneath it, once every six seconds at full speed, so quarter speed is the one to watch.

What you are looking at

Each satellite sits 36,000 km above one point on the equator and sees a little less than half the globe. Where two of them see the same place, their pictures are averaged, weighted by how steeply each one looks down: a place directly under a satellite is all that satellite, a place seen at a grazing angle counts for less, and past 70° off nadir a satellite is not used at all. The Seams button tints the picture by which satellite it came from, so the blend can be inspected rather than taken on trust.

The seams and the gaps

the poles

Nothing looks down on the poles

Every one of these satellites is over the equator. Poleward of about 60° they see the Earth edge-on, and past 70° off nadir their picture is not used. The polar caps and the top and bottom of the flat map are the basemap, dimmed and standing still: there is no weather there on this page. A polar-orbiting satellite would fill it, at twice a day rather than every ten minutes.

two kinds of picture

Real colour and painted cloud

GOES-East, GOES-West and MTG deliver a finished colour picture. Himawari and Meteosat-9 are used here through their infrared channel alone, which measures cloud-top temperature. Cold is turned into an opaque white cloud and laid over the basemap. That rendering cannot see warm low cloud, fog or marine stratocumulus, and it paints very cold ground, the Tibetan plateau or Antarctic air over Australia in winter, as if it were cloud. The seam between the two kinds runs down the Indian Ocean and across the western Pacific.

the overlaps

Blended, not cut

Where two satellites see the same place at a slant, their pictures are averaged. Colour balance differs a little between agencies and the horizon of each disk is hazy, so the mid-Atlantic and the central Pacific are slightly softer than the middle of a disk. Time is exact to the minute for four satellites; Meteosat-9 runs every fifteen minutes and the nearest picture is used, so its clouds step a little.

the cadence

Every ten minutes, all of it

One frame every ten minutes, 24 frames a second: four hours of weather a second, a day in six seconds. The archive on this page is three days; NASA GIBS keeps about thirty-five and EUMETSAT keeps years, so the same page can hold a month once it is worth the download.

Why a globe

A flat map lies about the poles and hides the fact that the atmosphere is one connected thing. On the sphere the tropical storms of the western Pacific, the fronts crossing the Atlantic and the great swirls of the Southern Ocean are all one fluid on one ball, and the day-night line is a line the planet turns through. That is the picture a model of how skies change should have in its head; this page is for checking that the data can be assembled into it.
