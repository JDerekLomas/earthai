The cloud layer

One day of real cloud, every ten minutes, over an Earth that is lit by arithmetic.

The only thing on this globe that changes from one ten-minute picture to the next is the cloud, so the cloud is the only thing that is a video. Everything else you see, the land and sea, the day and the night, the line of dusk, the city lights, the shadow a cloud throws on the ground by its own measured height, is computed while you watch from where the sun is. Drag to turn it, scroll to zoom. Switch to sun-fixed and the daylight stands still while the Earth turns through it.

What is real and what is computed

The clouds are real. They come from the satellite's own measurements, two channels of the sixteen: how much sunlight a place reflects at 0.64 µm, and how cold it looks at 10.3 µm. By day both are used; by night only the temperature, so night cloud is softer and the page shows it dim, where the eye cannot tell. Each pixel's measurement is compared with what that place looks like with no cloud at all, its darkest and warmest self over the day, and the difference is the cloud, a single number from clear to opaque. No lighting is in that number, which is why there is nothing to mismatch where two satellites will meet.

What is missing

the seams

Five satellites, checked with arithmetic

Where two satellites both see a place at a decent angle, each has its own opinion of the cloud there, and because both opinions are the same physical number they can be compared: the mean difference in opacity across each overlap is printed below, measured over every frame. The two Meteosats come through EUMETSAT's map server as pictures rather than numbers, so their greys are calibrated against a neighbour (MTG against GOES-East over the Atlantic, Meteosat-9 against MTG over Africa) by matching distributions before they are used. Everywhere no satellite sees is drawn honestly clear: the Coverage button shows where that is.

the poles

Nothing looks down on the poles

Every geostationary satellite sits over the equator and sees the Earth edge-on past about 60° of latitude, so past 66° there is no cloud on this page at all. The polar caps are the basemap, lit but weatherless.

the clear-sky guess

Where the method is wrong

"What this place looks like with no cloud" is taken from one day of pictures, so a place under cloud all day has no clear reference and its cloud reads thinner than it is. Bright ground (the Atacama, Greenland's ice) is handled by that reference and stays ground; the sun's glint on the sea is switched to the infrared reading alone, so a calm sea at noon is not white. Very cold ground at night, and thin high cloud after dark, are the known failures of an infrared-only night.

the height

Every cloud has a top, and it is measured

The infrared band reads the temperature of whatever it sees, and the air cools by about 6.5 °C for every kilometre up, so a cloud top 65 °C colder than the ground beneath it is about ten kilometres high. That number, 0 to 16 km for every cloudy pixel, rides in the same clip as the opacity and does three things here: the shadow a cloud throws is as long as its height and the sun's angle say (drawn at three times its true length, because at ten kilometres a pixel a true one is under a pixel for all but the tallest storms); high cloud is a little cooler in colour and keeps the sun for some minutes after the ground below it has gone dark, by the geometry of being higher; and where the top steps up toward the sun, that edge is lit. Two things are wrong with it, and stay wrong: thin cirrus reads too low, because its temperature is a mix of the cold cloud and the warm ground showing through, so a veil at twelve kilometres can come out at five; and a real atmosphere is not a standard one, so the heights are good to a kilometre or two, not a hundred metres.

between frames

Ten minutes, in motion

A real frame every ten minutes, twelve of them a second at full speed: two hours a second, a day in twelve seconds. Between two frames the cloud has moved, typically one to three pixels, and a cross-fade would show it fading from one place to the next. So the motion between each frame and the next is measured (optical flow, on the frames themselves) and travels with the clip, and the Blend button carries each frame along it: the older frame is pushed forward, the newer pulled back, and the two are mixed. Nothing is invented; where the motion estimate is wrong the cloud ghosts a little. Switch it off to see the frames step.

the resolution

Ten kilometres per pixel, and closer over the Americas

The whole-Earth cloud is drawn at ten kilometres per pixel, 4096 of them around the equator. Zoom in over the Americas and the page switches to tiles cut from GOES-East at its own resolution, 2.4 kilometres per pixel, decoded in the browser only for the part of the world in view (each tile is its own small video, fetched a second at a time). The zoom stops where a pixel of the finest cloud available under the view would be two device pixels wide, so the cloud is never magnified into mush over a basemap that is still sharp: about two and a half times closer where the tiles exist than elsewhere. The other four satellites see just as finely; their tiles are not built yet, and a browser without WebCodecs stays on the whole-Earth clip.
